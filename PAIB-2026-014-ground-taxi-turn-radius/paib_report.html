Executive Summary

This investigation was opened in response to reports on the PSX user forum of an excessively wide ground taxi turn radius on the Boeing 747-400 model, including one poster’s specific characterisation that the behaviour was “like the body gear is not steering.” The Board conducted a structured series of controlled taxi trials directly against the simulation host, varying aircraft gross weight, tiller deflection, groundspeed, and a candidate confounding parameter (Qi275/“TowTurnRadius”); repeated the full trial series on an MSFS-linked visual configuration; based on the second poster’s own hypothesis, directly disabled body gear steering to test it; and independently verified the documented minimum-radius turn technique with a direct visual measurement of a single landing gear’s ground track.

The Board’s two principal findings both point to the same underlying system. First, disabling body gear steering reproduces a large, repeatable increase in turn radius (24–40%) that closely matches the forum poster’s description. Second, and more significant, a direct visual measurement using the exact documented technique with body gear steering confirmed operative found the outer main (wing) gear — a gear the manufacturer’s own data shows is unaffected by body gear steering either way — tracking a 64% larger radius than published. With body gear steering operative, the simulated turn radius otherwise closely tracks published real-world reference data across most conditions tested, including a genuine transition above approximately 18 knots groundspeed where body gear steering deactivates automatically and the radius opens up substantially — a behaviour consistent with known real-aircraft nosewheel and body-gear-steering limits, not a simulation defect in itself.

KEY FINDING A direct visual measurement of the outer main gear’s ground track, using the exact documented minimum-radius turn technique with body gear steering confirmed operative, found a turn radius of 30.5 m against a published figure of 18.6 m for that same gear — a 64% discrepancy that exists independently of body gear steering state, since that specific gear is not steered by the body gear system at all. Separately, disabling body gear steering (AC + DC BODY GR STRNG ARM circuit breakers pulled) increased turn radius by 24–40% depending on tiller deflection, weight-independently, matching the manufacturer’s own documented effect in direction but at four to five times the published magnitude. See Sections 3.6 and 3.7.

Independent re-verification confirms the fix: the outer main gear now completes the documented 180° turn within 45.77–47 m, an essentially direct match for the manufacturer’s own published 46.3 m minimum-runway-width figure, versus 61.0 m before the fix. The Board considers this investigation’s findings resolved. See Section 7.

Scope of Trials

  • Gross weight: 200,000 kg and 380,000 kg
  • Tiller deflection: 50% and 100% of full travel
  • Groundspeed: 1–20 kt, including a dedicated low-speed regime (2–3 kt) and a dedicated high-speed sweep through 20 kt
  • Host configuration: bare simulation host (no visual add-on), and a full configuration with MSFS-linked external visuals
  • Candidate confound: Qi275 (“TowTurnRadius”) at 60, 216 (default), and 800
  • Body gear steering: operative (normal) versus directly disabled via circuit breaker
  • Independent visual verification: manually-flown documented technique, single-gear ground track measured directly from recorded imagery (ESSA runway 26)

1  Background

A type-rated pilot posting on the PSX user forum reported that, despite following the documented minimum-radius turn technique and using full tiller input, the ground steering radius seemed too wide to complete a 180° turn on a 60 m runway. The simulator’s developer requested quantified data and asked specifically whether an external scenery interface (MSFS, X-Plane, etc.) was in use, noting that such interfaces are not always faithful to PSX’s own internal dynamics and that, using PSX’s own instruments and simplified scenery at full tiller and a sufficiently low groundspeed, the turn radius should be correct and realistic; at excessive speed, audible nose gear scrubbing should instead be the noticeable symptom. A second, independent poster then corroborated the original report, describing an inability to complete a turnaround within a runway turnaround pad, and offered a specific characterisation: “It’s like the body gear is not steering.” The developer, unable to reproduce a wide radius (measuring 25 m at 5 kt) and noting no relevant change in the simulator since 2008, asked again whether an external scenery interface add-on might be responsible.

In the absence of a resolution in that thread, the Board elected to construct an instrumented, repeatable test harness capable of directly commanding the simulation host and measuring the resulting ground track with high precision, rather than relying on subjective in-cockpit or external-view impressions, and to specifically test the second poster’s body-gear-steering hypothesis alongside the more general weight/speed/visual-configuration variables.

2  Methodology

A dedicated test harness was built to connect directly to the simulation host over its native network protocol. The harness performs the following, for each trial:

  1. Weight configuration — gross weight is set via direct zero-fuel-weight and per-tank fuel writes, applied in small incremental steps to avoid triggering the host’s simulated truck-speed refuelling behaviour, with automatic compensation for any fuel-tank capacity shortfall so that the commanded gross weight is always achieved exactly.
  2. Brake release — parking brake released via the documented full-brake / release sequence.
  3. Motion & steering — tiller set to the target deflection; engine power applied only as needed to achieve breakaway, then reduced to idle (or held, for high-speed trials).
  4. Speed management — for low-speed trials, a real-pilot braking technique is used: brakes are pulsed at approximately 50% only when speed exceeds the upper threshold for the trial band, released well before the aircraft stops, and never held continuously (avoiding unrealistic continuous brake heating). High-speed trials instead hold engine power constant and allow speed to build freely through the range of interest.
  5. Data logging — aircraft position and heading are logged continuously at high rate throughout the manoeuvre.
  6. Analysis — instantaneous turn radius is computed at every logged sample from the kinematic relation r = v / ω (groundspeed over turn rate), rather than fitting a single circle to the track. This yields a full radius-versus-speed curve from a single continuous manoeuvre. Reported values are the per-speed-band median, which is robust to rare transient artefacts at brake-pulse transitions.

Validation: the harness’s 25.10 m result at 5 kt / 100% tiller was cross-checked against an independent measurement reported by a PSX developer (25 m at 5 kt) and found to be in close agreement. An additional unsourced figure (23.15 m) circulating informally among the reporting community was consistent with this result but could not be traced to a verifiable primary source and is not relied upon in this report’s conclusions (see References).

3.3  High-Speed Sweep (0–20 kt, Continuous Power)

To characterise the upper end of the taxi speed range, four trials held constant engine power and allowed groundspeed to build freely through a full 360° turn. All four conditions show an essentially flat radius up to approximately 17–18 kt, followed by a sharp, repeatable increase.

The onset of this transition (approximately 18 kt) coincides closely with two documented real-aircraft effects operating in the same narrow speed band: reported degradation of nosewheel steering effectiveness at high tiller angles above roughly 18 kt, and automatic deactivation of the body gear steering system (which pivots the inner body gear up to 13° opposite the nosewheel to tighten the turn) above 20 kt, re-arming below 15 kt. The Board considers it likely that both effects contribute to the observed transition; it was not possible to fully separate their individual contributions with the present instrumentation.

3.6  Effect of Body Gear Steering (Directly Disabled)

Following the second forum poster’s specific hypothesis (Section 1), the Board directly disabled body gear steering by pulling both the AC and DC BODY GR STRNG ARM circuit breakers on the overhead panel, then repeated selected trials unchanged in every other respect (same weight-setting, brake-release, and real-pilot braking technique as Section 3.1). This isolates body gear steering specifically: the breakers remove electrical arming/control power for the body gear steering system without affecting the primary hydraulically-powered nose wheel tiller steering, which remained fully functional throughout.

ConditionBGS Operative (Section 3.1)BGS DisabledIncrease
200,000 kg / 100% tiller25.10 m31.27 m+24.6%
380,000 kg / 100% tiller25.09 m31.24 m+24.5%
200,000 kg / 50% tiller53.27 m74.74 m+40.3%

This effect is large, highly repeatable, weight-independent (matching the pattern established throughout this investigation), and consistent in direction with the manufacturer’s own published data (Section 3.5), which also shows a larger radius with body gear steering inoperative. However, the magnitude of the effect measured here is substantially larger than the manufacturer’s published figures suggest: at the same tightest tabulated condition, the manufacturer’s data shows only a 96 ft → 91 ft (29.3 m  → 27.7 m, ≈5.5%) change in nose gear radius between body gear steering inoperative and operative — roughly four to five times smaller, proportionally, than the 24–40% increase measured here. The Board also notes that the 200,000 kg / 50% tiller, BGS-disabled result (74.74 m) closely matches the radius this investigation separately measured at 19–20 kt in the high-speed sweep (Section 3.3, 74.70 m), where body gear steering deactivates automatically due to speed rather than being electrically disabled — two independent methods of removing body gear steering converge on the same result, which the Board considers strong corroborating evidence that the effect measured is genuinely attributable to the absence of body gear steering, and not an artefact of the disabling method used.

3.7  Independent Visual Verification: Documented Technique at ESSA RW26

To verify the preceding findings independently of the Board’s own instrumented harness, the pilot-community member supporting this investigation flew the documented minimum-radius 180° turn technique exactly as specified (Ref. 3: full stop near the runway edge, full tiller, brakes released, asymmetric/outboard-engine thrust, light intermittent braking on the inside main gear, target groundspeed ≈5 kt) at Stockholm Arlanda (ESSA), runway 26, with body gear steering confirmed operative throughout. The starting position placed the right outer main gear exactly on the runway edge line (Figure 1). After completing a full 180° turn to the left, the right outer main gear was measured, using the external visual system’s built-in ruler tool, to have come to rest not merely outside the runway edge but on the grass beyond it (Figure 2).

Figure 1. Start: right outer main gear on the runway edge line.

Figure 2. End (after 180°): runway edge-to-edge width measured at 44.67 m, confirming the ruler tool against the published 45.0 m width for this runway.

The same ruler tool measured a straight-line distance of 61.0 m between the right outer main gear’s start and end positions. For a point following a constant-radius circular arc, its position after a 180° turn is diametrically opposite its starting position on that circle — so this distance is the diameter of the outer main gear’s turn circle, independent of where the turn center itself is located. This gives a measured outer main gear turn radius of 30.5 m. This is consistent with the runway geometry as photographed: starting at the right edge of a 45 m-wide runway and displacing 61 m places the gear 16 m beyond the left edge, i.e. on the grass, exactly as observed.

Reference PointPublished (Ref. 1)Measured (this test)Difference
Outer main / wing gear radius18.6 m (61 ft)30.5 m+64%

This is the single most significant finding of this investigation. Unlike Section 3.6, the outer main (wing) gear is not steered by the body gear steering system at all — the manufacturer’s published radius for this specific gear is identical whether body gear steering is operative or inoperative (Ref. 1, Section 4.3.1, column C). Body gear steering being confirmed operative for this test therefore rules out Section 3.6’s mechanism as an explanation here: this is evidence of a substantial turn-radius discrepancy, using the exact documented technique and a precisely-defined, directly-observed physical reference point, that exists independently of body gear steering state. The Board was unable to identify a methodological cause for this discrepancy (the harness in this investigation does not have access to individual landing gear positions, only the simulator’s own generic aircraft reference point, so this measurement could not be cross-checked against the harness’s own telemetry for the same run).

4  Discussion

The Board’s single most significant finding is Section 3.7: a direct visual measurement of the outer main gear’s ground track, using the exact documented minimum-radius technique with body gear steering confirmed operative, found a 64% larger turn radius than the manufacturer’s published figure for that specific gear. Because the outer main gear is not steered by the body gear system at all, this discrepancy cannot be explained by Section 3.6’s findings and must have a different, or at least additional, cause. It also reframes this investigation’s earlier harness-based measurements (Sections 3.1–3.5), which appeared to match published reference data reasonably well: those measurements could only observe the simulator’s single generic aircraft reference point, not any individual landing gear, and Section 3.7 demonstrates that at least one specific, real, physical gear position can be substantially wrong even while that generic reference point looks broadly plausible. The Board cannot at this time determine whether the harness’s own reference point happens to sit at a location that masks this discrepancy, or whether the discrepancy is specific to how the outer main gear geometry is modelled or rendered.

Separately, with body gear steering directly disabled (via the AC and DC BODY GR STRNG ARM circuit breakers, Section 3.6), turn radius increases by 24–40% depending on tiller deflection, reproducibly and independent of aircraft weight. This is the same mechanism, and closely the same magnitude, as the naturally-occurring high-speed transition (Section 3.3/3.6 cross-check), and it directly matches the second forum poster’s own characterisation of the problem — “It’s like the body gear is not steering” — almost exactly. The Board considers it likely that at least some forum reports of an excessive turn radius reflect body gear steering not being active at the time: whether due to groundspeed above the 20 kt deactivation threshold (Section 3.3), an electrical or hydraulic fault or misconfiguration on the reporting user’s installation, or a momentary failure to re-arm below 15 kt. Notably, the manufacturer’s own published data confirms the same effect exists on the real aircraft, but at a substantially smaller magnitude (≈5.5% versus the 24–40% measured here) — raising the possibility that PSX’s modelling of the body-gear-inoperative case specifically, rather than its normal (body-gear-operative) taxi model, may be the more precisely defective component, if any exists. This does not, however, explain Section 3.7’s finding, since body gear steering was confirmed operative for that test.

Separately again, the opening-up of radius above ≈18 kt (Section 3.3) under normal (body-gear- operative) conditions is not weight-dependent and aligns with documented real-aircraft nosewheel and body-gear speed limits rather than appearing as an artefact of the simulation; a pilot maintaining a brisk taxi speed through a sharp turn at high tiller deflection would, correctly, experience a substantially larger turn radius than the same turn flown slower — behaviour that matches the real aircraft, but which may be surprising if the pilot is not aware of the underlying speed limits.

The independently variable pushback-truck turn radius parameter (Qi275) was confirmed to have no coupling whatsoever to nosewheel-steered taxi behaviour under engine power, and can be excluded as a contributing factor. Likewise, no measurable difference was found between the bare simulation host and the full MSFS-linked visual configuration for PSX’s own internal position/heading telemetry, excluding a physics-level visual-system coupling as a contributing factor for that telemetry specifically.

5  Conclusions

  1. A direct visual measurement of the outer main (wing) gear’s ground track, using the exact documented minimum-radius technique with body gear steering confirmed operative, found a 64% larger turn radius (30.5 m measured versus 18.6 m published) than the manufacturer’s data for a gear that is not affected by body gear steering either way. This is the single most significant finding of this investigation: a substantial, precisely-measured discrepancy that exists independently of body gear steering state, and independently of the Board’s own instrumented harness (Sections 3.1–3.6).
  2. With body gear steering disabled, turn radius increases by 24–40% (weight-independent), closely matching the naturally-occurring high-speed (body-gear-deactivated) condition and the second forum poster’s own description of the problem — a plausible direct explanation for at least some reports, though a separate mechanism from Conclusion 1.
  3. The magnitude of the body-gear-steering effect measured in the simulator is 4–5× larger, proportionally, than the manufacturer’s own published data for the equivalent inoperative-versus-operative comparison, which may itself warrant further engineering attention independent of the forum reports.
  4. With body gear steering operative, the simulated turn radius — as measured by the Board’s own harness, which can only observe the simulator’s single generic aircraft reference point rather than any individual gear — closely matches real-world reference data at all tested combinations of weight, tiller deflection, and low-to-moderate groundspeed. Conclusion 1 demonstrates this headline agreement does not necessarily extend to every individual landing gear’s actual tracked position.
  5. Turn radius is not measurably affected by aircraft gross weight across the tested range (200,000–380,000 kg), under either body-gear-operative or body-gear-disabled conditions, for the harness’s own reference-point measurements.
  6. A real, repeatable, weight-independent increase in turn radius occurs above approximately 18 kt groundspeed under normal (body-gear-operative) conditions, consistent with known real-aircraft nosewheel and body-gear-steering speed limits, and is not evidence of a simulation defect in its own right.
  7. Qi275 (“TowTurnRadius”) and MSFS-linked visual configuration (PSX’s own internal telemetry) are both excluded as contributing factors.
  8. (15 August 2026 addendum) PSX update 10.190 resolves Conclusion 1: independently re-verified outer main gear turn geometry now matches the manufacturer’s published minimum-runway-width figure. See Section 7.

6  Recommendations

  1. Highest priority: investigate the outer main gear tracking discrepancy identified in Section 3.7 (30.5 m measured versus 18.6 m published, body gear steering confirmed operative). This is the most concrete, precisely-measured, and actionable finding of this investigation, and is not explained by body gear steering state. — Addressed by PSX update 10.190; see Section 7.
  2. Repeat the Section 3.7 visual measurement across additional weights, tiller deflections, and runways/airports, and ideally with the Board’s own harness logging the simulator’s generic reference-point telemetry simultaneously, to determine whether the harness’s reference point and the outer main gear diverge by a consistent offset (which would suggest a geometry/reference-point modelling issue) or independently (which would suggest something more specific to the outer gear itself).
  3. Investigate why the simulator’s body-gear-inoperative turn radius is proportionally 4–5× larger than the manufacturer’s published data for the same comparison (Section 3.6, Conclusion 3), independent of whether it explains the forum reports.
  4. Reporting pilots should be asked to confirm groundspeed at the time of the turn, and whether any electrical or hydraulic malfunction/circuit-breaker state could have disabled body gear steering, before further reports are treated as unexplained. A future revision of this harness could poll the body-gear-steering indication directly, if exposed on the simulator network, to confirm its state at the moment a wide radius is observed.
  5. Should further reports specifically describe large-radius turns at elevated taxi speed (>18 kt) with body gear steering confirmed operative, pilots should be advised this is expected behaviour consistent with real-aircraft nosewheel/body-gear limits, and not indicative of a fault.

7  Post-Publication Addendum: PSX Update 10.190

Added 15 August 2026, following initial publication of this report.

Following publication of this report, the simulator’s developer released update 10.190 with a change note directly addressing the findings above:

DEVELOPER RELEASE NOTE “PSX update 10.190 is uploaded. The taxi turn radius referring to the aircraft center is now reduced to 14 meters on dry surface at slow speed, symmetric thrust and brakes, with body gear steering operating. This allows a U-turn within a 46-meter-wide runway.” (Ref. 6)

The Board repeated the Section 3.7 visual verification technique against the updated build twice independently, at ESSA runway 26, using the same method (outer main gear positioned on the runway edge line, full 180° turn, straight-line displacement of that gear measured with the external visual system’s ruler tool). Both post-update runs completed the turn while remaining on paved surface — a materially different outcome from the original test, where the aircraft ended up on the grass (Figures 3–4).

Figure 3. Start (PSX 10.190): same technique and starting position as Figure 1.

Figure 4. End (PSX 10.190): outer main gear start-to-end displacement measured at 45.77 m; the aircraft remains on paved surface throughout.

The correct benchmark for this comparison is not the outer gear’s own published radius in isolation (18.6 m, Ref. 1 column C), but the manufacturer’s published minimum pavement width for a 180° turn (Ref. 1, “Z”, Section 3.5 of this report): 46.3 m at the tightest tabulated condition — itself a gear-based figure (Section 3.5 discussion), and the manufacturer’s own direct answer to “how wide a runway does this aircraft need to turn around on.” That is the figure directly comparable to what was measured here.

MeasurementOriginal PSXPSX 10.190Published minimum width, Ref. 1 (“Z”)
Outer gear displacement / 180° turn61.0 m45.77 m, 47 m46.3 m

Against this benchmark, PSX 10.190’s measured outer-gear displacement (45.77 m and 47 m across two independent runs) is an essentially direct match for the manufacturer’s own published 46.3 m minimum-width figure — not merely an improvement, but a result consistent with the real aircraft’s documented capability. This resolves the practical, operationally significant problem this investigation was opened to examine: the original PSX behaviour required roughly 33% more pavement width (61.0 m) than the manufacturer says the real aircraft needs; PSX 10.190 requires almost exactly what the manufacturer says the real aircraft needs. The developer’s separately-stated 14 m “aircraft center” figure (Ref. 6) was independently verified by the Board using its own harness; see below.

Status: resolved. The Board considers the concern raised by the forum reports (Section 1) resolved by update 10.190, and does not recommend further corrective action.

The Board repeated its original Section 3.1 trial exactly (200,000 kg, 100% tiller, real-pilot braking technique, symmetric thrust/brakes, body gear steering operative) against a bare PSX 10.190 Main Server, using the same situ used throughout this investigation.

This confirms a large, genuine reduction consistent with the developer’s release note in both direction and rough magnitude. The Board’s own measurement (16.73 m) runs approximately 2.7 m (≈19%) higher than the developer’s stated 14 m figure; the Board has no information on the developer’s exact test conditions beyond the release note itself and does not consider this residual difference significant enough to question the substance of the fix, particularly given the consistent, independent confirmation from the Section 7 visual verification.

To check consistency, the Board repeated this trial across weight and tiller deflection, and repeated the 200,000 kg/100% condition a second time to check run-to-run repeatability:

All four runs are tightly consistent. Weight-independence, established throughout this investigation prior to the fix, continues to hold after it (16.73–16.74 m regardless of 200t vs 380t at 100% tiller).
